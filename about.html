About this guide

A catalogue of New England ski areas from roughly 650 feet of vertical upward, built on one rule: every number on this site either has a source and a date attached to it, or it is a dash. There is no third category, and nothing here has been rounded into existence to fill a gap.

What this is

Most ski-resort listings are one of two things: a marketing page written by the resort, or an aggregator that scraped the marketing page. Both will tell you a mountain has excellent terrain for all abilities. Neither will tell you that the black diamonds ski like blues, that the summit lift goes on wind hold most Saturdays, or that the bumps you came for get groomed flat at nine in the evening.

So this guide keeps two kinds of content strictly apart and labels which is which. Facts — vertical, acreage, trail counts, snowfall averages, snowmaking coverage, pass affiliation, prices — carry a source URL and the date somebody last checked them. Editorial — the overview, the crowd notes, the difficulty read, the mogul and park write-ups, the best-for and watch-out-for lists — are opinions, written in our own words, and are meant to read like opinions. If a mountain is icy, crowded or overpriced, the editorial says so.

The cutoff is roughly 650 feet of vertical, which is where a New England hill starts being somewhere you drive to rather than somewhere you learn on. That line is a judgement call and a few areas sit near it either way.

Where the numbers come from

Sources are used in this order, and every resort record says which one supplied each figure:

  1. The resort's own site. Its mountain-stats or about page is the primary source for elevations, acreage, trail and lift counts, snowmaking and terrain splits. It is also the source most likely to be flattering — several of the disputes noted on detail pages exist because a resort's own figure is larger than anyone else's.
  2. State ski associations — Ski Vermont, Ski New Hampshire, Ski Maine and the rest — for figures a resort does not publish, and for cross-checks such as which areas actually operate at night.
  3. Independent aggregators, chiefly OnTheSnow, plus ZRankings and PeakRankings where they cover a mountain. Used when the first two have nothing, and used with care: an aggregator page that quotes two different values for the same figure is common, and at least one pair of same-named mountains has had its records mixed together at the source.
  4. OpenStreetMap for lift geometry and summit coordinates, via the Overpass API. Where a resort publishes no usable summit coordinate, the top station of its highest chairlift is used instead — which is the point a forecast should be anchored to anyway.

Where a resort and an independent source disagree and both are defensible, the detail page shows both figures side by side rather than quietly picking a winner. Where nothing could be sourced, the field is a dash — never a guess, never a number carried over from a similar mountain. Fields that are blank because the resort genuinely does not publish them are marked as such and distinguished from fields nobody has checked yet.

Weather

Forecasts come from Open-Meteo and are fetched by your browser, live, each time you open a page — nothing is stored on a server, because there is no server. Results are cached in your browser for thirty minutes so that moving between pages does not re-request the same forecast.

Every forecast is anchored to the summit rather than the base lodge. On a New England mountain two thousand feet of elevation is routinely the difference between snow and rain, and a base-lodge forecast will tell you the wrong one on exactly the days you care most.

Snow reports, and how they are fetched

Where a mountain publishes a machine-readable report, a scheduled job reads it and stores the figures. That job runs every two hours from November through April and does not run at all in summer. It follows a few rules, and they are not negotiable:

  • Every request identifies this project by name and links to this page, so a resort's operations team can tell what the traffic is and ask us to stop rather than silently blocking an address.
  • robots.txt is honoured, and no host is contacted more than once every two hours. Requests are never made to one host in parallel.
  • Only numbers and short condition labels are stored — base depth, new snow, lifts and trails open. Prose from a resort's report is never stored and never displayed.
  • Every figure shown carries a relative "as of" time and a link to the report it came from. Anything older than twenty-four hours is not shown as a number at all; the page links out to the official report instead.
  • When a fetch fails, the site says so and links to the resort's own report. A stale base depth presented as today's is worse than no base depth.

Not every mountain has an automated report. Smaller areas frequently publish conditions only on social media, and those are linked rather than parsed.

The opinions

Editorial text is written for this site and is not copied, paraphrased or machine-rewritten from a resort's marketing copy or from any review site. Where the guide summarises somebody else's review, it says whose, links to it, and gives the gist in our words with their published rating if they publish one.

Some of it reports what skiers say rather than what can be verified — that a mountain's ratings run soft, that a particular liftline bumps up first. Anything in that category is attributed as consensus, not stated as fact.

Nobody pays to be here and nothing on this site is an affiliate link. There are no photographs, which is deliberate: a resort's own photography is its own, and a stock picture of a chairlift tells you nothing a number does not.

Corrections

Figures go out of date every autumn, resorts get bought, lifts get replaced, and some of what is here is simply wrong. Corrections are welcome and a source link is worth more than an argument.

Colophon

Static HTML, one stylesheet and a handful of ES modules. No framework, no bundler, no build step, no tracking, no cookies, no analytics, and no server — the pages you are reading are files, and everything dynamic on them happens in your browser. The resort data is a directory of JSON files that the pages read directly; the site cannot show you a figure that is not in one of them.
